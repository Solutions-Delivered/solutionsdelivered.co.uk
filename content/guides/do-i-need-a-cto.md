Usually, no. If you run a business with 1 to 20 staff, what you're missing
isn't a full-time executive on a six-figure salary — it's a few hours a
month of senior technical judgement from someone who isn't trying to sell
you anything. The rest of this page explains why, and is honest about the
businesses where a real, full-time CTO genuinely is the right call.

## What's actually missing

You're the person who has to be everything for the business, and an expert
in everything. Somewhere in the pile of decisions you make on no sleep, a
run of technical ones keeps turning up: which accounting software to move
to, whether that agency's quote for a new website is fair, whether your
supplier's "we take security seriously" answer means anything, what to do
about the AI tools everyone keeps mentioning.

None of these questions are hard for someone who does this for a living.
They're hard for you because you don't have anyone whose job it is to
answer them without an angle. Every vendor pitching you software, every
agency quoting you a website, every consultant selling "AI transformation"
has a reason to tell you yes. What's missing isn't more information — it's
one person on your side who has no stake in the answer.

This is also why the questions never really go away. You can Google your
way to a defensible answer on any one of them given enough evenings. What
you can't get from a search engine is someone who remembers the answer you
settled on last time, knows how your systems actually fit together, and
will tell you when a new decision contradicts an old one.

That gap is real, and it's common. Government research into UK business AI
adoption found that 71% of businesses cite "no identified need" as the
reason they haven't adopted AI — but the qualitative interviews behind that
figure tell a different story: businesses that struggle to picture where AI
would actually help, not businesses that have genuinely concluded they don't
need it (DSIT, [AI Adoption Research](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research),
2026). "No identified need" is very often "nobody whose job it is to
identify it."

## What a technical leader actually does at this size

Strip away the job title and the work is specific and recurring:

- **Vendor and quote judgement.** Is this software the right tool, or the
  one the salesperson pushed hardest? Is this development quote reasonable
  for the work described?
- **Tooling and system decisions.** What you run your business on, and
  whether to change it, made by someone who has seen the alternative fail
  elsewhere.
- **Security posture.** Not "are we secure" as a yes/no, but which risks
  are worth fixing this quarter and which are noise.
- **Continuity.** What happens if the one person who understands your
  systems leaves, and whether you'd notice the gap before it became a
  crisis.
- **AI judgement calls.** Which tools are worth a serious look, which are
  noise, and what a sensible policy on using them with client data looks
  like.

That's a real job. It is not, for most businesses this size, a full-time
one.

## What a full-time CTO actually costs

The median advertised salary for a Chief Technology Officer in the UK was
£100,000 a year, based on vacancies posted in the six months to 25 August
2026 ([ITJobsWatch](https://www.itjobswatch.co.uk/jobs/uk/chief%20technology%20officer.do)).
That's salary only — add employer National Insurance, pension
contributions, equipment, and the cost of actually finding and hiring the
right person, and the real annual cost sits well above the headline figure.

For that money you get one person, full-time, employed. Most 1 to 20 staff
businesses don't have enough technical decision-making to fill a working
week — they have a steady trickle of decisions that need judgement, not
a job that needs filling. Paying full-time-executive money for a few hours
a month of actual demand is the expensive way to answer the question.

There's also the hiring itself to account for: recruiting at this level
takes months, a bad hire costs you the salary and the lost time, and a
good one will get bored running a technology function sized for one person
a fraction of the week. None of that shows up in the headline salary
figure, and all of it is avoided by paying for judgement in the quantity
you actually use. We've written a fuller breakdown of what the fractional
and outsourced options cost by comparison in [how much a fractional CTO
costs in the UK](/guides/fractional-cto-cost-uk).

## When you genuinely need a full-time CTO

Some businesses do need one, and telling you otherwise would be bad advice
dressed up as a sales pitch. A full-time CTO is the right answer when:

- **Technology is the product.** You sell software, an app, or a platform,
  and technical decisions are made daily, not monthly.
- **You have an in-house development team.** Someone needs to manage,
  mentor and be accountable for engineers day to day. That's a management
  job, not an advisory one, and it doesn't work part-time.
- **Technical risk is existential, not occasional.** If a bad technical
  call could sink the business by next quarter rather than cost you a
  wasted contract, you need someone in the room every day, not once a
  month.

If that's your business, go and hire properly — a fractional or outsourced
arrangement will under-serve you, and no amount of good advice from us
changes that. The rest of this page is for everyone else.

## The middle option

For most owner-managed businesses, the answer sits between "nobody" and
"a full-time executive": one senior person who owns your technology
decisions, vendor relationships, security posture and continuity, on an
advisory retainer sized to how much judgement you actually need — not a
headcount you have to justify every month. It's the same category of role
as a fractional or virtual CTO, described in language that fits how UK
owner-managed businesses actually talk about the problem: an [outsourced
IT director](/outsourced-it-director).

In practice that means someone who is available when a decision comes up —
a supplier contract to check, a security question from an auditor or a
customer, a call on whether a piece of software is worth the money — rather
than someone you have to keep permanently busy to justify. It's advisory
first: the aim is that you make better decisions faster, not that you hand
the decisions over.

## FAQ

**Isn't this just a fractional CTO with a different name?**
Functionally, yes — same category of role, described in the vocabulary UK
owner-managed businesses actually use rather than the US-imported
"fractional CTO" label most providers borrow from venture-funded
start-ups.

**We already pay an IT support company. Isn't that covered?**
Usually not. Most IT support contracts cover keeping things running —
helpdesk tickets, break-fix, patching. Almost none of them cover deciding
what you should be running in the first place, or telling you honestly
when a supplier's quote or a new tool isn't worth it. Those are different
jobs, and the gap between them is exactly where this sits.

**How much does it cost?**
It's sized to what you need rather than sold as a fixed package — an
advisory retainer, not a salary. The full cost picture, including what
comparable providers publish, is in [how much a fractional CTO costs in
the UK](/guides/fractional-cto-cost-uk).
