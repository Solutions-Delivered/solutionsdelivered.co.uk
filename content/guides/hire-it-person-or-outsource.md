Usually neither, on its own. The first IT hire is the most expensive way to
answer the wrong question, because most owners are trying to fill one role
that is actually three separate jobs: helpdesk, projects, and decisions. Hire
one generalist to cover all three and you overpay for the parts they're weak
at and get nothing for the part that matters most. Split the jobs first, then
decide route by route.

## What job are you actually hiring for?

Owners searching "should I hire an IT person or outsource" are usually
picturing one role. There are three, and they want different people, different
contracts, and different money.

**Helpdesk.** The printer that won't print, the password reset, the laptop
that won't boot, the new starter who needs an account by Monday. Reactive,
constant, and genuinely commodity work — it doesn't need your best thinker,
it needs coverage and a fast response. This is what an MSP (managed service
provider) is built for.

**Projects.** The migration, the new finance system, the website rebuild, the
office move that needs everything re-cabled and re-configured. Defined start,
defined end, defined deliverable. This is contract work — you don't need it
on the payroll permanently, you need it done well once.

**Decisions.** Which software to buy, whether a supplier's quote is fair,
whether you're actually secure or just told you are, what to do about AI,
what happens if a key supplier vanishes overnight. Ongoing, judgement-based,
and — this is the part owners miss — **nobody's job**. A helpdesk hire won't
touch it; a project contractor is scoped to their one project and gone. Most
1-20 staff businesses have no one whose job it is to own these calls, so they
get made badly, late, or by whoever's loudest in the room (often a vendor's
salesperson).

That gap is the actual problem behind "should I hire an IT person or
outsource." A single hire, however good, is built to plug one or two of the
three — usually helpdesk and a bit of projects. The decisions gap stays open
whichever way you answer the headline question.

## What does each route actually cost?

Numbers first, because owners routinely underestimate the salaried option by
ignoring everything except the payslip.

| Route | Covers | What you're actually paying |
|---|---|---|
| Salaried IT manager | Helpdesk + some projects + informal decisions | £55,000 median gross salary for a UK IT Manager, six months to 25 August 2026 ([ITJobsWatch](https://www.itjobswatch.co.uk/jobs/uk/it%20manager.do)) |
| MSP / helpdesk contract | Helpdesk, patching, monitoring | Priced per user or per device, scales with headcount — a fixed, predictable line rather than a fixed person |
| Advisory retainer | Decisions only | Scoped to the engagement — a few hours a month of senior judgement, not a full-time cost |

The salary figure is only the start of the salaried-hire cost, and this is
where the comparison usually goes wrong. On top of £55,000 you add employer
National Insurance — 15% on earnings above the £5,000 secondary threshold for
2026/27 ([HMRC rates and thresholds for
employers](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rates-and-thresholds-for-employers-2026-to-2027)),
which is roughly £7,500 on that salary alone. Then employer pension
contributions on top of the statutory minimum, a laptop and software licences,
recruitment fees or the weeks of your time spent interviewing, and — the one
nobody prices — the four to eight weeks after they start when they're still
learning your systems and delivering less than a contractor would from day
one. A realistic all-in figure for one salaried IT manager sits well north of
£65,000 in year one, before you've asked them a single strategic question.

An MSP contract avoids most of that on-cost because you're buying a service,
not a person — no recruitment, no notice period, no single point of learning
curve. What it doesn't buy is anyone accountable for whether your technology
spend or your security posture is actually right; that's not the job you're
paying for.

An advisory retainer is the cheapest of the three in absolute terms precisely
because it's scoped to the one job that doesn't need full-time coverage:
someone senior enough to make and review decisions doesn't need to be in the
building forty hours a week to do it well.

## What happens if your one IT hire leaves?

There's a fourth cost the table doesn't show, and it only shows up the day
someone hands in their notice. When a single person has been quietly
absorbing all three jobs — the passwords, the vendor relationships, the "why
we set it up this way" — their knowledge leaves with them. Nothing was ever
written down because there was never a second person to write it down for.
The replacement search starts from zero, usually while something urgent is
already broken. This single-point-of-failure risk is the strongest argument
against solving "helpdesk, projects and decisions" with one generalist hire,
however good they are while they're still there: concentrating all three
jobs in one person concentrates all the risk in one person too.

## How do most businesses actually combine these three routes?

The honest answer to "hire or outsource" is neither in isolation — it's a
combination, and which combination depends on size and how much is already
broken.

- **Under about 10 staff:** an MSP for helpdesk, contractors called in per
  project, and an advisory retainer for the decisions that would otherwise
  fall to whoever's most confident with a laptop. No salaried IT role at all.
- **10-20 staff, growing:** the same shape, but the advisory relationship
  starts doing more — reviewing the MSP's renewal quotes, vetting the next
  system purchase, sanity-checking what "we should use AI for this" actually
  means for your business. Still no full-time hire, because the volume of
  helpdesk tickets rarely justifies one before 20+ staff.
- **Past 20-25 staff, or after a bad year:** this is usually where a first
  IT hire starts to make sense — enough volume that a dedicated person earns
  their keep on helpdesk and projects alone. Even then, most businesses this
  size keep an outside advisory relationship for the decisions a single
  employee, however senior, shouldn't be marking their own homework on
  (is the security posture actually adequate, is this supplier quote fair).

If you're weighing [an outsourced IT director](/outsourced-it-director)
against a salaried hire specifically, the deciding question is usually
[whether you need a CTO at all](/guides/do-i-need-a-cto) — most 1-20 staff
businesses need a few hours a month of senior judgement, not a full-time
executive.

## Common questions

**Can one MSP contract cover the decisions job too?**
Some will say yes and sell it as part of the package, but the incentives
don't line up: an MSP is paid to keep tickets resolved and infrastructure
running, not to tell you their own renewal quote is high or that you don't
need the upsell they're pitching. Decisions need someone without a stake in
the answer.

**Isn't a generalist IT hire cheaper than paying for three things
separately?**
On paper, sometimes. In practice you're paying one salary to get partial
coverage of three different skill sets, and the part that suffers first is
almost always the strategic one — a helpdesk-minded hire under day-to-day
pressure rarely has the time or the remit to step back and question the
technology spend.

**How do I vet whoever ends up owning the decisions job, if it's not an
employee?**
The same way you'd vet any consultant before handing over that much trust —
[there's a checklist for exactly this](/guides/vet-an-it-consultant).

**What if we already have an IT person and I think this is the gap they're
not covering?**
That's common, and it isn't a reflection on them — it's usually a scope
problem, not a competence one. Adding an outside advisory relationship
alongside an existing hire, rather than instead of one, is one of the more
common shapes this takes.
